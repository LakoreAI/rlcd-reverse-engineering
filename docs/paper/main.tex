\title{Dissecting RLCD: What Reinforcement Learning Does (and Doesn't) Do for Calibrated Typed Decisions}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Le Duc Minh}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Independent Researcher\\
Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam\\
minh.leduc.0210@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
``Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions'' (RLCD) is the training
method behind typed-decision models that return a probability distribution
over a closed set of options. The method behind TypeSafe's Jev is
unpublished; the only open reproduction, Laya, adds a noisy-logit
policy-gradient term to ordinary soft cross-entropy (CE). This report asks
what that RL term actually does. It is a score-function
(evolution-strategies) estimate of the gradient of a noise-smoothed proper
scoring rule. For the log score, its optimum is provably sharper than the
target once the noise is removed for inference, and the gap grows with the
noise scale~$\sigma$. A 13-run ablation that reproduces Laya's fine-tune on
the typed-decisions benchmark shows the effect at full scale: at fixed
$\sigma\in\{0.5,1,2\}$, the temperature fitted to binary questions rises from
1.2 to 1.7 and NLL from 0.871 to 0.928, while accuracy stays flat. At Laya's
own annealed schedule the RL gradient is 8--87$\times$ the CE gradient, yet it
changes nothing measurable, and CE alone is at least as good on every metric
we track. The benchmark's hard labels turn out to be the teacher's argmax,
which makes hard-label ECE reward over-sharpening; we propose scoring against
the target distribution instead. Finally, the only change that improves
accuracy is not in the loss at all: matching the checkpoint's documented
1024-token sequence budget raises accuracy from 0.782 to 0.789.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
calibration, proper scoring rules, reinforcement learning, typed decisions,
non-autoregressive models, evolution strategies
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

A new class of ``System One'' models answers structured questions about
unstructured state in a single forward pass. No text is generated. Each
answer is a probability distribution over a closed, typed set of options: a
\emph{choice} among named options, an ordinal \emph{score}, or a binary
\emph{noul} probability. TypeSafe's Jev~\cite{typesafe_blog} is the most
visible example. Its central promise is calibration: an answer given with 0.8
confidence should be right about 80\% of the time. The method credited for
it, RLCD~\cite{rlcd_explained}, has no published paper, reward function,
dataset or calibration figure~\cite{typesafe_blog,techcrunch_jev}.

Laya~\cite{laya_repo} is an open, transparent attempt at the same interface.
It publishes code, checkpoints and an evaluation on the
\texttt{LocalLLaMA/typed-decisions} benchmark~\cite{typed_decisions_ds}. Its
training loop adds noise to the logits, rewards the noisy distributions with a
proper scoring rule, and applies a REINFORCE-style
update~\cite{williams1992reinforce} next to a soft CE term. Laya's own README
reports a raw ECE of 0.466, falling to 0.081 once temperature scaling is
applied. On its face, then, the RL objective does not produce calibration by
itself.

This report asks what that RL term actually does. There are four findings.
\begin{enumerate}
\item It is an evolution-strategies
  estimator~\cite{salimans2017es,nesterov2017random} of the gradient of a
  noise-smoothed scoring rule, and as $\sigma\to 0$ it returns the CE gradient
  the model already has (Sec.~\ref{sec:analysis}).
\item For the log score and binary questions, the smoothed optimum is sharper
  than the target at noise-free inference, with a gap that increases strictly
  in~$\sigma$; the same direction holds numerically for Laya's full reward and
  $K\le 77$ options (Sec.~\ref{sec:analysis}, \ref{sec:e1}).
\item A 13-run ablation of the loss reproduces Laya's fine-tune
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:e2}). The predicted bias appears in full at fixed
  $\sigma\ge 0.5$, while at Laya's annealed schedule the RL term dominates the
  update and still buys nothing over CE-only.
\item The benchmark's gold labels are the teacher's argmax, so hard-label ECE
  \emph{improves} as a model over-sharpens; we propose a target-referenced ECE
  that measures the bias rather than rewarding it.
\end{enumerate}
Nothing in this report claims to know how Jev is built. Statements about Jev
come from TypeSafe's public claims and are labelled as inference.

\section{Background and Related Work}

Calibration is a statement about a predictor's own confidence. With
$c=\max_i p_i$ as the confidence, the predictor is calibrated if
$\Pr(\text{correct}\mid c)=c$. Expected calibration error (ECE) estimates the
gap with $B$ equal-width bins~\cite{naeini2015ece,guo2017calibration}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{|S_b|}{n}\,\Bigl|\,\overline{c}_{S_b}-\overline{a}_{S_b}\Bigr|,
\label{eq:ece}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{a}$ is the empirical accuracy in the bin; we use $B=15$ on
$c=\max_i p_i$. Temperature scaling~\cite{guo2017calibration} divides the
logits by a scalar $T$ fitted by NLL on held-out data. A value $T>1$ says the
raw outputs were over-sharp.

A scoring rule $R(q,t)$ is strictly proper if $\E_{y\sim t}R(q,y)$ is uniquely
maximized at $q=t$~\cite{gneiting2007scoring}. Laya combines three of them:
the log score $\sum_i t_i\log q_i$ (its negative is soft CE), the spherical
score $\langle t,q\rangle/\|q\|$, and, for ordinal questions, the ranked
probability score (RPS)~\cite{epstein1969rps}. Brier~\cite{brier1950} is
$\|q-t\|_2^2$. Distilling onto teacher distributions~\cite{hinton2015distilling}
with soft CE therefore already optimizes a strictly proper score.

Three post-training families are relevant here. RLHF optimizes a learned
preference reward~\cite{christiano2017rlhf,ouyang2022instructgpt}, and
post-training of this kind has itself been observed to degrade
calibration~\cite{kadavath2022language,openai2023gpt4} and, when the reward is
a proxy, to reward-hack it~\cite{gao2022overoptimization}; RLVR
optimizes a verifiable reward~\cite{lambert2024tulu3}, often with a group-mean
baseline as in GRPO~\cite{shao2024grpo}; RLCD, as publicly described, rewards
calibrated typed distributions~\cite{rlcd_explained}. Perturbing logits and
following the score function is Gaussian smoothing, which is to say evolution
strategies~\cite{salimans2017es,nesterov2017random,mohamed2020mcgrad}.

A second family smooths the \emph{target} rather than the logits. Label
smoothing and mixup replace one-hot targets with softer ones, and both are
known to reduce over-confidence and improve calibration, including for
pre-trained encoders~\cite{muller2019labelsmoothing,thulasidasan2019mixup,desai2020calibration}.
That is the opposite direction from this report: those methods change what the
model is asked to fit, and inference reads the same (soft) prediction it was
trained on. Laya smooths the logits at training time and then \emph{removes}
the noise at inference, so the model fits a smoothed objective but reports a
sharp one. The next section makes that gap precise.

Open reproductions also point past Laya's recipe. Verdict-2.0, a 151M
ModernBERT-base decision model, reports higher accuracy than Laya on this same
benchmark ($0.771$ vs.\ $0.766$) with far lower ECE ($0.0144$ vs.\ $0.213$)
using a cross-entropy plus Brier composite loss~\cite{openjev}; a larger
multi-domain model uses the same $L_\mathrm{CE}+\lambda L_\mathrm{Brier}$ form
at scale~\cite{von}. Both replace the RL term rather than add to it, which is
the direction the experiments below support.

\section{Laya From the Inside}
\label{sec:laya}

Each question becomes its own sequence:
\texttt{[CLS] <type> question: <instr> [SEP] [MASK] opt$_0$ [MASK] opt$_1$ \dots\ [SEP] <state> [SEP]}.
A ModernBERT-large encoder~\cite{warner2024modernbert} runs over it, followed
by a type embedding, two pre-norm transformer layers and an MLP scorer. The
scorer reads one logit $z_i$ from the hidden state at each option's
\texttt{[MASK]}, and inference reports $p=\softmax(z/T)$. We rebuilt this
model and checked tensor by tensor that Laya's released weights load into our
version (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

Algorithm~\ref{alg:laya} restates the training step from Laya's
typed-decisions fine-tune notebook~\cite{laya_repo}. Two details in it are
easy to miss, and both change the gradient. The spherical weight is 0.75, not
the library default of 0.5. And the group-centred advantages are divided by
their own standard deviation; using the raw-reward standard deviation instead
shrinks the RL gradient by about 90$\times$ in our tests.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Laya's RLCD training step (one batch).}
\label{alg:laya}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE rows with targets $t$, noise $\sigma$, samples $G{=}4$, weights $w_\mathrm{rl}{=}w_\mathrm{ce}{=}1$
\STATE $z \leftarrow f_\theta(x)$ \COMMENT{masked logits, $B\times K$}
\FOR{$g=1$ to $G$}
  \STATE $e_g\sim\N(0,\sigma^2 I)$;\ \ $\epsilon_g\leftarrow e_g-\bar e_g\mathbf{1}$ \COMMENT{zero mean over valid options}
  \STATE $q_g\leftarrow\softmax(\mathrm{sg}(z)+\epsilon_g)$;\ \ $r_g\leftarrow R(q_g,t)$
\ENDFOR
\STATE $a_g\leftarrow r_g-\tfrac1G\sum_{g'}r_{g'}$;\ \ $a\leftarrow a/(\mathrm{std}(a)+10^{-6})$
\STATE $L_\mathrm{RL}\leftarrow-\tfrac{1}{GB}\sum_g a_g\log\N(\mathrm{sg}(z)+\epsilon_g\mid z,\sigma^2 I)$
\STATE $L_\mathrm{CE}\leftarrow-\tfrac1B\sum t^\top\log\softmax(z)$
\STATE step on $w_\mathrm{rl}L_\mathrm{RL}+w_\mathrm{ce}L_\mathrm{CE}$ (clip 1.0, AdamW)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Here $R(q,t)=\sum_i t_i\log q_i+0.75\,\langle t,q\rangle/\|q\|-\mathbb{1}[\text{score}]\,\mathrm{RPS}(q,t)$,
and $\mathrm{sg}$ is a stop-gradient. The noise scale $\sigma$ is annealed
linearly from 0.4 to 0.1 over four epochs. A temperature per question type is
fitted afterwards on a held-out slice; an earlier version fitted it on
training items, which issue \#186~\cite{laya_issue186} records.

\section{Analysis of the RL Term}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{The RL term estimates a smoothed objective}

Let $P=I-\tfrac1K\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$ and $\epsilon\sim\N(0,\sigma^2P)$,
the zero-mean-projected noise of Algorithm~\ref{alg:laya}. Define
\begin{equation}
J_\sigma(z)=\E_{\epsilon}\bigl[R(\softmax(z+\epsilon),t)\bigr].
\label{eq:jsigma}
\end{equation}
The projected noise lies in $\mathrm{range}(P)$, so the pseudoinverse score
under $\N(0,\sigma^2P)$ reduces to $\epsilon/\sigma^2$ and
$\nabla_z\log\N(\mathrm{sg}(z)+\epsilon\mid z,\sigma^2 I)=\epsilon/\sigma^2$,
the gradient of $L_\mathrm{RL}$ is
\begin{equation}
-\nabla_z L_\mathrm{RL}=\frac{1}{GB\,\sigma^2}\sum_{g}a_g\,\epsilon_g,
\label{eq:es}
\end{equation}
a group-baselined Monte-Carlo estimate of the Gaussian-smoothing identity~\cite{nesterov2017random,mohamed2020mcgrad}
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\nabla_z J_\sigma(z)&=\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\,\E\bigl[R(\softmax(z+\epsilon),t)\,\epsilon\bigr]\\
&=P\,\E\bigl[\nabla_u R(\softmax(u),t)\big|_{u=z+\epsilon}\bigr],
\end{split}
\label{eq:stein}
\end{equation}
where the second equality is Stein's lemma. For the log score,
$\nabla_u R=t-\softmax(u)$, so as $\sigma\to0$ the RL term estimates
$t-\softmax(z)$, the negative CE gradient itself. In other words, it is a
noisy version of a gradient CE already supplies exactly, plus a deviation
controlled by $\sigma$.

\subsection{The smoothed optimum is over-sharp}

For the log score, $J_\sigma$ is concave (an average of concave log-softmax
terms), so its maximizers are the stationary points
\begin{equation}
\E_\epsilon\bigl[\softmax(z^\star_\sigma+\epsilon)\bigr]=t .
\label{eq:stationary}
\end{equation}
The \emph{noise-averaged} prediction matches the target, but inference reports
the \emph{noise-free} $\softmax(z^\star_\sigma)$. For binary questions (noul,
$K{=}2$) the consequence can be proved exactly. With projected noise, the
logit gap $d=z_1-z_2$ receives $\eta=\epsilon_1-\epsilon_2\sim\N(0,2\sigma^2)$,
and \eqref{eq:stationary} becomes $F_\sigma(d^\star):=\E[s(d^\star+\eta)]=t_1$
with $s$ the logistic function.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:binary}
Let $K=2$, the log score, and $t_1\in(\tfrac12,1)$. For every $\sigma>0$
there is a unique maximizer $d^\star_\sigma$. It satisfies
$s(d^\star_\sigma)>t_1$, and $s(d^\star_\sigma)$ is strictly increasing in
$\sigma$ (symmetrically for $t_1<\tfrac12$).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $g=s-\tfrac12$. It is odd, increasing, and concave on $[0,\infty)$,
with $g''(x)=s(1-s)(1-2s)$ odd and negative for $x>0$. Fix $d>0$ and pair
$\pm\eta$. If $|\eta|\le d$, concavity gives
$g(d+\eta)+g(d-\eta)\le 2g(d)$. If $|\eta|>d$, then
$g(d+|\eta|)+g(d-|\eta|)=g(|\eta|+d)-g(|\eta|-d)$. This is the increment
of a concave function over an interval of length $2d$ starting at
$|\eta|-d>0$, so it is at most $g(2d)-g(0)\le 2g(d)$, strictly for
$\eta\neq0$. Hence $F_\sigma(d)<s(d)$ for all $d>0$. The map $F_\sigma$ is
continuous and strictly increasing, with $F_\sigma(0)=\tfrac12$ and
$F_\sigma(\infty)=1$, so $F_\sigma(d)=t_1$ has a unique root $d^\star>0$,
and $s(d^\star)>F_\sigma(d^\star)=t_1$. For monotonicity, write
$\eta=\tau Z$ with $\tau=\sqrt2\sigma$. By Stein's lemma,
$\partial_\tau\E[g(d+\tau Z)]=\tau\,\E[g''(d+\tau Z)]$. Because $g''$ is odd
and negative on $(0,\infty)$, and the Gaussian centered at $d>0$ puts more
mass at $+x$ than at $-x$, this expectation is negative. So $F_\sigma(d)$
decreases in $\sigma$ at fixed $d$. Keeping $F_\sigma(d^\star_\sigma)=t_1$
therefore requires $d^\star_\sigma$ to increase with $\sigma$, and $s$ is
increasing.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[size of the bias]
A second-order expansion gives
$\E[\softmax(z+\epsilon)]=\softmax(z)+\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\sum_{jk}P_{jk}\partial_j\partial_k\softmax(z)+O(\sigma^4)$.
The optimum therefore deviates from $t$ by $O(\sigma^2)$. Under Laya's
schedule ($\sigma\le0.4$, ending at 0.1) the deviation is 6--100$\times$
smaller than at $\sigma=1$. This predicts that Laya's recipe is nearly
unbiased but gains nothing over CE, which is what Sec.~\ref{sec:e2}
finds.
\end{remark}

For $K>2$ and for Laya's full reward we rely on numerics
(Sec.~\ref{sec:e1}). Temperature scaling can undo much of the distortion: at
the optimum, noise-free outputs are approximately the target sharpened, and a
uniform $T>1$ reverses most of that.

\subsection{Variance and advantage normalization}

After normalization the advantages are $O(1)$ whatever the reward scale, so
\eqref{eq:es} has norm $\propto\|\epsilon\|/\sigma^2\propto 1/\sigma$. As
$\sigma$ anneals, the RL term grows relative to CE. It is estimated from only
$G{=}4$ samples, and the batch-wide standard deviation rescales the effective
learning rate from step to step. The group mean includes each sample itself,
which shrinks the estimate by $(G-1)/G$. None of this changes the smoothed
optimum, but it determines how noisy the path to it is
(Sec.~\ref{sec:e3}).

\subsection{E1: Toy smoothing bias}
\label{sec:e1}

We maximize \eqref{eq:jsigma} directly by reparameterized gradients (Adam,
4000 steps, 4096 noise samples per step), which removes estimator noise so
that only the smoothed objective's optimum is measured. For $t=(0.7,0.2,0.1)$
and the log score, the noise-free $\max_i\softmax(z^\star)_i$ is 0.700, 0.723,
0.780 and 0.905 at $\sigma=0,0.5,1,2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:e1}). Laya's full reward
sharpens a little more: 0.923 as a choice question and 0.932 as a score
question at $\sigma=2$. Sweeping $K\in\{2,5,20,77\}$, peaked and near-uniform
Dirichlet targets, and both rewards gives 16 configurations, and in every one
of them $\mathrm{KL}(t\,\|\,p^\star)$ increases monotonically in $\sigma$. The
effect is largest for peaked mid-$K$ targets and smallest for near-uniform
$K{=}77$.

\subsection{E2: Loss ablation on real data}
\label{sec:e2}

We replicate Laya's typed-decisions fine-tune on rented GPUs. Every run starts
from \texttt{convaiinnovations/laya}~\cite{laya_hf}, trains for 4 epochs at
effective batch 64, and uses AdamW with learning rate $2.5\cdot10^{-5}$ on the
encoder and $10^{-4}$ on the head, cosine decay, gradient clipping at 1.0,
$G{=}4$, $\sigma$ annealed from 0.4 to 0.1, and bf16. The loss is the only
thing that changes: CE-only ($w_\mathrm{rl}{=}0$), Laya's RL+CE, RL-only
($w_\mathrm{ce}{=}0$), RL+CE at fixed $\sigma\in\{0.25,0.5,1,2,3,4\}$, and an
E4 sweep of the reward terms. CE-only, RL+CE, RL-only and every $\sigma\le2$
were run with three seeds; the rest with one. All runs share a fixed budget
and are evaluated on their final weights, with temperature fitted per (type,
option-count bucket) on a held-out 10\% of the \emph{training} cases. The test
set holds 400 cases and 2000 questions. Our RL+CE replica reaches accuracy
$0.773\pm0.002$ and Brier $0.054\pm0.001$, against 0.766 and 0.062 in Laya's
README. One further run (``CE-only, 1024/256'') doubles the input budget to
the 1024 tokens the checkpoint card documents, against the 512 the port
otherwise uses.

One caveat about the benchmark changes how the numbers should be read. The
gold label of each question equals $\argmax_i t_i$ on 98.4\% of test rows: it
is the teacher's mode, not an outcome drawn from $t$. Scored as a predictor,
the teacher itself has hard-label ECE 0.326, at mean confidence 0.659 and
accuracy 0.984. A model that reproduced $t$ perfectly would look badly
calibrated, and over-sharpening would \emph{lower} its hard-label ECE by
pushing confidence toward accuracy. We therefore measure the bias against the
target distribution, using a target-referenced ECE that swaps the 0/1 hit of
\eqref{eq:ece} for the target's probability of the chosen option:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ECE}_t=\sum_{b}\frac{|S_b|}{n}\Bigl|\,\overline{c}_{S_b}-\overline{t[\argmax p]}_{S_b}\Bigr|,
\label{eq:softece}
\end{equation}
which is 0 when $p=t$. Alongside it we report the sharpness gap
$\overline{\max p}-\overline{\max t}$, NLL and Brier against $t$, the fitted
$T$, and, as the plan requires, raw and post-temperature hard-label ECE.
Fig.~\ref{fig:rel} puts the two references side by side.

range --- NLL $0.866,0.871,0.890,0.928,0.956,0.987$ and $T_\text{noul}$
$1.14,1.16,1.38,1.72,2.08,2.42$ --- while accuracy is flat ($0.764$--$0.776$).
The three-seed spread at $\sigma{=}1$ is an order of magnitude smaller than
these steps. Over the same runs hard-label ECE falls ($0.115,0.083,0.060$),
exactly as the caveat predicts, and temperature scaling recovers most of the
NLL loss ($0.928$ to $0.863$ at $\sigma{=}2$) --- systematic sharpening, not a
degraded ranking.

At Laya's own schedule the RL term does nothing useful. RL+CE and CE-only are
indistinguishable in sharpness ($-0.017$ vs $-0.016$) and in fitted $T$.
CE-only wins on NLL for every seed ($0.861$ vs $0.866$, and still $0.857$ vs
$0.863$ after temperature scaling), on Brier ($0.052$ vs $0.054$) and on
accuracy ($0.782$ vs $0.773$). RL-only (three seeds now) is the least accurate
($0.769$) but is not over-sharp. This fits the $O(\sigma^2)$ remark: at
$\sigma\le0.4$ the bias is too small to see, and what is left is estimator
noise.

The sharpening is not uniform. Split by target-entropy tercile
(Table~\ref{tab:entropy}), every model, CE-only included, is under-sharp on
peaked targets and over-sharp on flat ones, as if regressing toward typical
confidence. Raising $\sigma$ adds a near-uniform upward shift across the
terciles. The near-zero mean gap of CE-only is the sum of two opposite
biases, not evidence that no bias exists.

The sequence budget is the only accuracy lever. The largest accuracy effect is
not in the loss at all. Matching the 1024-token budget the checkpoint card

(256), so questions with many options lost tokens. Continuing from the
already fine-tuned \texttt{laya-typed-decisions} checkpoint at the same 1024

starting point.

\subsection{E3: Gradient quality}
\label{sec:e3}

Every 10 optimizer steps we compare \eqref{eq:es} against the CE gradient,
both with respect to the logits and each drawn from a fresh noise sample
(Table~\ref{tab:e3}, Fig.~\ref{fig:grad}). Under Laya's schedule the RL
gradient is 8$\times$ the CE gradient at $\sigma{=}0.4$ and 87$\times$ at
$\sigma{=}0.1$, so before clipping it dominates the update; its alignment with
CE is only moderate (cosine $\approx0.65$). In the fixed-$\sigma$ runs, where
$\sigma$ is not entangled with training progress, the ratio scales as
$1/\sigma$ (ratio$\times\sigma\approx$ 5.2, 4.9, 4.3) and alignment falls from
0.60 to 0.52 to 0.37 as the smoothed gradient drifts from the CE gradient.
Size notwithstanding, the RL term leaves the final model about where CE alone
would leave it. Everything is consistent with a noisy but roughly unbiased
proxy for the CE direction at small $\sigma$.

\subsection{E5: Consistency probes}
\label{sec:e5}

On the $\sigma{=}2$ checkpoint we perturbed 500 test questions and re-scored
them. Option order is mostly handled: reversing the options changes the chosen
option on 6.6\% of rows (choice 2.0\%, score 14.0\%, noul 1.0\%; the score
figure is partly definitional, since reversing ordinal levels is not an
invariance). Appending a neutral suffix ``(restated)'' to every option changes
2.0\% of picks. Appending an unrelated passage to the state changes 13.4\% of
picks, the sensitivity to irrelevant context TypeSafe documents for
Jev~\cite{typesafe_docs}. A rough negation probe (negate the instruction and
swap the true/false wording) leaves a mean
$|p(\text{true})-p_{\text{neg}}(\text{false})|$ of $0.53$: the two wordings are
far from mutually exclusive.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
A second run of RL+CE seed 42 matched the first closely (NLL 0.864 / 0.867,
accuracy 0.772 / 0.772), though not bitwise. An earlier seed-42 run is a
different story: it was the first training job on a fresh machine, matched the
others exactly at step 1, and then diverged to accuracy 0.697 and NLL 0.936.
We could not pin down the cause; our best guess is the first
\texttt{torch.compile}/Triton build, since \texttt{gcc} had been installed
minutes earlier. We keep it out of the tables but archive it with the
results.

\section{Toward a Principled RLCD Pipeline}
\label{sec:principled}

If the goal is calibrated typed distributions, the analysis above suggests the
following. Begin with distillation: soft CE against targets from a controlled
data engine, with exact labels where the latent state is known, known
posteriors where noise is injected, and teacher ensembles otherwise. Save
reinforcement learning for what CE cannot do, such as non-differentiable
workflow rewards, sequential decisions with temporal-difference targets, and a
separate action/escalation head whose utility never reaches the probability
head. Then enforce calibration where it can be measured: mine (type, $K$,
length, language) buckets with high target-referenced error and generate more
data there; add consistency regularizers for negation pairs (which should sum
to one), option permutation and irrelevant context; and fit a temperature per
(type, $K$), treating a large fitted value as a sign that an upstream stage
distorted the distribution. Evaluation should always show raw
target-referenced calibration beside the post-temperature numbers, and should
use labels drawn from distributions, for instance multi-annotator sets such as
ChaosNLI~\cite{nie2020chaosnli}, whenever calibration on outcomes is claimed.
This last part is a proposal; it describes no existing system.

\section{Limitations}

Jev is closed. Sec.~\ref{sec:jev} infers from public claims and tests nothing
about Jev itself. The experiments use a single benchmark whose labels are
teacher modes, so small accuracy differences are weak evidence; the teacher's
self-agreement ceiling is 0.735, which is why we lead with proper scores and
fitted temperatures. Three configurations have a single seed. All runs start
from a checkpoint Laya had already trained with RLCD, which can only help
RL-only. Proposition~\ref{prop:binary} covers the log score at $K{=}2$; the
general-$K$ and full-reward cases rest on numerics. The E3 diagnostics are
taken at the logits, not the parameters. Model scale is fixed at 421M
parameters.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Laya's RL term optimizes a noise-smoothed proper score. As $\sigma\to0$ that
objective becomes the CE the model already minimizes; for $\sigma>0$ its
optimum is over-sharp at inference. We proved this for binary questions, saw
it numerically for Laya's full reward, and watched it hold at full scale:
fitted temperature and target-referenced calibration error both rise
monotonically with $\sigma$. At Laya's own small annealed $\sigma$, the RL term
dominates the gradient and still leaves calibration unchanged while proper
scores get slightly worse than CE-only. For Laya-style typed-decision models,
then, the calibration comes from distillation and temperature scaling, not
from the RL term. On benchmarks whose labels are teacher modes, hard-label ECE
should not be used to argue otherwise.

\section{Reproduction details}

Laya's \texttt{model.safetensors} holds 206 tensors. Of these, 201 map
one-to-one onto our model (encoder, head, type embedding, scorer); the other

loss scaling; a case-level instead of row-level calibration split; temperature
per (type, $K$-bucket) instead of per type; and one GPU with effective batch

\section{The RLCD loss (condensed from our port)}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
k   = mask.sum(-1, keepdim=True).clamp(min=2)
eps = torch.randn((G,) + z.shape) * sigma * mask
eps = (eps - eps.sum(-1, keepdim=True) / k) * mask
zs  = z.detach().unsqueeze(0) + eps
q   = torch.softmax(zs.masked_fill(~mask, -1e4), -1)
with torch.no_grad():
    r   = proper_reward(q, t, qtype, mask, w_sph=0.75)
    adv = r - r.mean(0, keepdim=True)
    adv = adv / (adv.std() + 1e-6)
logp    = -(((zs - z) ** 2) * mask).sum(-1) / (2 * sigma**2)
loss_rl = -(adv * logp).mean()
loss_ce = -(t * torch.log_softmax(z, -1)).sum(-1).mean()
loss    = w_rl * loss_rl + w_ce * loss_ce
\end{lstlisting}
